## Rates & Macro

The rate that prices far-future profit rose again, and this time it is back in the zone that matters. The inflation-stripped ten-year yield — the one that moves the AI names — sits at 2.31 percent, above the top of the 2.25-to-2.30 percent danger level it had slipped below on July 6. The nominal ten-year held at 4.56 percent, up one basis point on the day but eight over three sessions; the two-year, the market's read on the Fed, rose two points to 4.21 percent. On the rule that has framed this book since July 1, real-yields-back-in-the-zone should mean the AI trade de-rates. It did the opposite — the S&P closed at a record.

Read this as a one-session override, not an all-clear and not a repeal. Two of the year's largest spending commitments — Meta's roughly $145 billion capital plan and Micron's $250 billion US build — lifted expected AI cash flows by more than a few basis points of discount rate could subtract. And the yield move was the wrong kind to frighten stocks: an oil-and-Warsh term-premium push that equities read as transitory. The exit line still sits where it sat — the real ten-year above roughly 2.25-to-2.30 percent, or the two-year toward 4.4-to-4.5 percent. We are above the lower edge, not decisively through it; the June inflation report due July 14-15 is the swing, and a hot number re-arms the de-rate against a complex that just ignored it once. One tell says the calm is thin: the bond-market fear gauge (MOVE) jumped 10.7 percent while the stock-market fear gauge (VIX) fell 6.5 percent — bonds pricing rising rate risk, stocks pricing none.

## What Happened

### The record was memory's, not the GPU's

The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,543.55 on Thursday, up 0.81 percent, even as real yields sat back above their danger zone — the AI trade ignoring the rate math it obeyed a week ago. The lift was narrow and specific: an oversubscribed SK Hynix US listing forced institutions to chase AI-memory allocation, and Sandisk (SNDK) rose 7.6 percent, Micron Technology (MU) 4.5 percent, Western Digital 5.0 percent. The leaders sat it out — NVIDIA (NVDA) fell 0.7 percent and Taiwan Semiconductor (TSM) was flat, both trailing the chip tape by about three points. Two capital commitments fed the demand story underneath: Micron's $250 billion US build and Meta's ~$145 billion plan. This was a memory scramble, not a broad AI re-rating.

### Bloom Energy drew its first short report

Bloom Energy (BE), the fuel-cell maker and a portfolio holding up roughly 160 percent this year, was hit on Wednesday by a short-seller report from Hunterbrook Media alleging misstated financial results and a China-dependent scandium-oxide supply chain; the stock fell more than 8 percent on the report. Bloom filed a material-event disclosure with the SEC on Thursday that "categorically rejects" the claims as false and misleading and says its scandium supply is sufficient and "not dependent on China" — but it points investors back to its existing filings rather than offering new figures, so the allegation cannot be cleared from the rebuttal alone. A short thesis is a hypothesis, not a finding. The July 28 earnings print, and the accounting disclosures in it, is where this resolves.

### The Hormuz truce collapsed; oil's risk trigger fired

Iran's ceasefire was declared over across the window: Tehran struck three commercial vessels near the Strait of Hormuz, the US revoked the waiver letting Iran sell crude and hit roughly ninety military targets, and oil ran up about 10 percent over two sessions before easing. West Texas crude closed Thursday at $71.86, down 2.26 percent on the day but higher across the week; Brent settled at $76.10. The escalation flipped the oil story from June's supply-glut disinflation to a Hormuz risk premium, and it is the inflation-premium engine behind the three-day rise in yields. The graph's Hormuz-disruption signal moved to active. Energy shares fell with the barrel, not the geopolitics — for now the premium sits in the crude, not the producers.

## Source Tone

The three-session window told a "scare, shock, then capex-overpower" story. July 7 was risk-off inside tech — a report that DeepSeek is building its own inference chip, plus data that Chinese open-source models run 60-to-90 percent cheaper, crystallized an AI-economics fear that the whole "expensive chips forever" premise might be wrong. July 8 layered a genuine macro shock: the Iran ceasefire collapse, an oil spike, and hawkish Fed minutes that repriced a September hike to 70 percent. By July 9 the outlets converged on "AI optimism overpowers rates and geopolitics," powered by Meta's and Micron's spending headlines. The tone is constructive but conditional — this is the demand narrative beating the discount-rate and cost-deflation narratives in a single session, not resolving them. The real-yield exit zone was breached and ignored: the most important tell of the window, and the one most likely to matter later if the catalyst flow thins.
