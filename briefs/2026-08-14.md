## Rates & Macro

The ten-year Treasury yield, the rate that prices long-dated stocks, closed at **4.68 percent, up five basis points**. The two-year, the market's read on the Federal Reserve, rose two to **4.17 percent**. The inflation-stripped ten-year — the number that marks down far-future artificial-intelligence profits — rose two basis points to **2.41 percent**, against ten-year inflation expectations of **2.27, up three**. The gap between the two- and ten-year widened from 48 basis points to 51: a clean bear steepening, long end leading, front end anchored.

Friday hurt the AI book, but mildly and for an odd reason. Only two of the five basis points reached valuations; the other three were inflation expectations, which do not. ## What Happened

### We were wrong twice. One cause.

Thursday's brief made two errors and they share a root. It published Workday (WDAY), the workplace-software maker, up 17.78 percent as cause-not-established, our filings check verified-negative. Reuters ran the cause that same day: **Silver Lake is reportedly in talks to take Workday private**, then valued at about $43 billion. No price has been reported. The shares were halted intraday. The brief also published Coherent's (COHR) **$1,998.6 million raise as "one unexplained cash-flow line." It was NVIDIA.** A material-event filing dated 2 March names the buyer, the 7,788,161 shares, the $256.80 price and the $2 billion in cash. Our cause screen reads filings only. A take-private approach files nothing until it is signed; Coherent's disclosure sat five months back. One miss is an error. Two from one cause is a method.

### The software floor lasted one session.

Thursday's rally read the Workday approach as a private-equity floor under beaten-down enterprise software, and the read-across cohort ran hard. Friday took it back. **A 22-name software cohort fell 2.83 percent on average, with 21 of the 22 down** and the worst at 5.56; Workday itself gave back 3.76 percent. Two forces, both real. The ten-year rose five basis points, which marks down the longest-duration cash flows in the market, and seat-priced software is the longest. On top of that a one-day merger trade unwound. Our own news desk carried Thursday's cohort percentages inside a Friday file; every one of them flips sign at Friday's close, and none reached this page. A valuation floor that lasts one session is not a floor.

### Same day, same chain, opposite cash.

Two suppliers to the same artificial-intelligence build filed annual reports on Friday and their cash statements point opposite ways. Coherent (COHR), the optical-components maker, grew revenue 22.5 percent and lifted gross margin 233 basis points. Its **operating cash flow fell 87.4 percent to $79.5 million and free cash flow came in at minus $1,023.4 million**. Western Digital (WDC), the disk-drive maker, grew 35.7 percent, lifted gross margin **1,007 basis points**, and turned **$3,929 million of operating cash and $3,511 million free**. Same day, same year, same demand. The difference is where the demand went: Coherent into work-in-progress, up 88.9 percent, and $1.1 billion of plant; Western Digital into the bank. Strip Coherent's payables build and its operating cash flow is minus $669 million. AI demand is not one trade.

### Applied Materials guided faster. It fell.

Applied Materials (AMAT), the largest maker of chip-production equipment and not a holding, fell 5.12 percent the day after a record quarter. Revenue was **$9,115 million, up 25 percent**; adjusted earnings per share $3.50, up 41. The guide is the striking part — **$10,250 million plus or minus $500 million, which against the year-ago quarter's $6,800 million is 50.7 percent.** That is an acceleration, not a raise. Margins expanded on every line, gross from 48.8 to 50.3 percent and operating from 30.6 to 33.7. A flat-margin story circulated widely and both our desks carried it; the filing says the opposite. What did not expand was cash: **net income up 43 percent against free cash flow up 14.** Three record prints sold in three sessions, all three on cash.

### The consumer missed. Yields rose anyway.

July retail sales fell **0.6 percent against expectations of a 0.1 percent gain** — the worst month since May 2025 — with online sales down 2.2 percent and vehicles down 1.8. Ninety minutes later the University of Michigan's preliminary August sentiment printed **51.0 against 54.5 expected**, its current-conditions component down 16 percent. Weak consumption normally pulls yields down. The ten-year rose five basis points, and inflation expectations supplied three of the five. The survey names its own cause: **the Iran war, through fuel, energy and food prices.** That is the same shock that has refining margins at a record, arriving on the household side of the same invoice. Walmart, Target, Home Depot and TJX all report the July quarter next week. The supply shock has found the demand side.

## Cascade Watch

### Small caps made a high while the index fell

- **What happened:** The S&P 500 fell 0.17 percent while its median constituent rose 0.05 and its equal-weighted mean rose 0.085 — a gap of **−0.255 points, the second negative reading in four sessions** after 11 August's −0.844 — and the Russell 2000 closed at a new 52-week high, verified against raw history rather than assumed.
- **Why it happened:** A five-basis-point steepening led by the long end repriced long-duration cash flows down and short-duration, inflation-passing ones up; the sellers were large and concentrated, the buyers were broad.
- **How it matters:** This is mega-cap de-grossing, not a market decline, and two facts stop it being read as risk-off — the speculative-growth fund fell 1.80 percent *while* small caps made a high, and the fear gauge fell 2.60 percent while the crash-hedge gauge rose 2.97. The buyer rotated within risk; he did not leave.

### The refiners are in the graph. The margin is not.

- **What happened:** Valero, Phillips 66 and Marathon Petroleum became tracked records this run, and the gasoline margin widened a third consecutive session, from $50.13 to $51.33 a barrel — while **not one US refiner filed anything at all, for a second consecutive session.**
- **Why it happened:** Our one commodity record carries a single crude price and all nine of its links point at the crude side, so it cannot express a margin; the macro record that would has two appearances one day apart against a seven-day minimum, and cannot be created before 20 August.
- **How it matters:** The largest identified profit pool in the energy market is still invisible to every instruction and link we run — and the null result is the evidence, since a foreign-capacity cause predicts exactly no American filings.

### A billion sold, $1.6 million bought

- **What happened:** Across 188 insider transaction filings the tracked cohort sold **$1,112,925,399** and bought **$11,610,402** — and once Intel's chief executive's $10 million "purchase" is recognised as almost certainly participation in his own company's share sale at the $95.00 offer price, genuine discretionary buying for the week was **$1.6 million**.
- **Why it happened:** Most of the selling is pre-planned rather than informed, and the cleanest proof is a null result: five Cisco insiders filed sale notices the day after an 8.40 percent fall, and every plan behind them was adopted between December and February. **Nobody sold ahead of that print.**
- **How it matters:** A 700-to-1 ratio is a distribution week, not a signal about any one name — but two rows do carry information, because both were discretionary: a Seagate executive vice-president selling $6.78 million into the cohort's second-biggest rise, and a chief financial officer at Tidewater selling with no plan behind him.

### Our own rule sorts on job title, not on money

- **What happened:** A **$478.8 million** disposition by CoreWeave's largest outside holder, filed on the session itself with no pre-planned sale plan, was graded medium — while a **$1.01 million** chief-executive sale at Diamondback was graded high.
- **Why it happened:** The materiality test has an arm for top executives and an arm for vice-presidents and no arm for large owners, so the biggest transaction of the week fell through the gap; a second instance the same week did the same thing on a $7.2 million same-day exercise-and-sell.
- **How it matters:** Our filings desk reported the defect instead of quietly re-bucketing the row, which is why we can see it — a 474-fold inversion means the insider census is currently sorted by who someone is rather than by how much they sold, and the fix is the rule owner's, not this run's.

### Seven links are overdue and none was written

- **What happened:** Seven candidate macro themes — among them the split between who monetises the AI build and who pays for it, the memory-price cascade, and the chip-equipment cycle — have now met every creation rule, some for over a hundred days, and **none has been created.**
- **Why it happened:** Writing one correctly means reading the underlying research for every connected name and verifying each link's mechanism from the source report. That is research, not bookkeeping, and it has never been budgeted inside a daily run.
