## What Happened

### Memory melted up. Its buyers did not.

Micron Technology (MU), the largest American maker of memory chips, jumped 11.3 percent to $835.56 at Tuesday's reopen — its first close above $800 and the cohort's largest one-day gain by a wide margin. Sandisk (SNDK) rode the same NAND-shortage bid, up 5.1 percent. UBS lifted its Micron price target to a Street-high $1,625, and contract pricing data confirmed server memory up 60 to 70 percent for the quarter. The tell was who did not follow. Nvidia (NVDA) rose only 0.4 percent and Microsoft (MSFT) fell, both lagging a chip rally they usually lead. Higher memory prices are a cost the cloud platforms pay, not a margin they earn. The memory makers stopped funding the rotation and started leading it.

### Oil broke. The whole complex moved together.

Brent crude fell 7.0 percent to $96.30, down 14 percent on the week, on a US–Iran framework to extend the ceasefire and reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Energy producers fell with it — the three-session split between crude and energy equities closed, and the group re-coupled to the crash. Airlines ran the other way on cheaper jet fuel, the travel index up 3.4 percent. Long-dated Treasury yields eased about seven basis points as the inflation premium came out. But nothing is signed. US forces struck inside southern Iran on Tuesday, destroying about sixteen mine-laying vessels near Hormuz, and the Revolutionary Guard vowed to retaliate. Iranian state media still disputes the two terms that matter — who controls the strait, and whether Tehran gives up its uranium. The cross-asset move was coherent; the deal under it is not.

### Software paid for the chip rally.

The bet against enterprise software came back hard after Friday's bounce proved a head-fake; the major names fell 2 to 3.4 percent together. Cloudflare (NET), the principal's holding in the group, dropped 2.9 percent — the day's worst Tier 1 performance, on the sector move rather than anything company-specific. Underneath the price action sat a concrete reason to doubt the AI-software trade. Microsoft confirmed it is winding down most of its Claude Code licences by the end of June on cost grounds. It is the first clear sign a large enterprise found agentic-coding tools cost more than the work they were meant to replace. Capital left the software and bought the chips it runs on.
