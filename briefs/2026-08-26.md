## Rates & Macro

The ten-year Treasury closed at **4.66%, up two basis points**, and the whole of that move was real. The inflation-stripped ten-year — the rate at which distant profits get marked down, and therefore the rate that prices the artificial-intelligence book — rose **two, to 2.34%**. The inflation half of the yield **did not move at all**, holding 2.32%. The two-year, the market's read on the Federal Reserve, rose **two to 4.19%**; the three-year rose four, the largest move anywhere on the curve. The gap between the two- and ten-year held at 47 basis points; the gap between the three-month bill at 3.85% and the ten-year is 81.

**A rise that is entirely real is a headwind for the artificial-intelligence book, and this one landed on a warm inflation print.** Our own exit calibration is a real ten-year holding above roughly **2.25–2.30%**. We are four to nine basis points through the top of it for a **sixth consecutive session, and it widened rather than narrowed.** The two-year at 4.19% is still well below the 4.4–4.5% line that would mark a hawkish repricing. These are calibrations, not promises.

Behind the curve sits an inflation print that ran hot on the headline and a consumer who stopped spending. July's preferred inflation gauge rose **3.7% against a 3.6% expectation** on the headline, with the core in line at 3.3%; **real consumer spending was essentially zero**, an $86bn rise in services against a $50bn fall in goods, and the saving rate is 3.0%. Second-quarter growth was confirmed at **1.5%**, unrevised and half the first quarter's pace, while corporate profits rose **$400.9bn**. Kevin Warsh, Federal Reserve chair since May, gives his first Jackson Hole keynote on 28 August. The consensus this brief has been carrying is wrong about the direction of the tail: **53% of surveyed economists expect rate rises over the next year**, the 29 July hold drew three dissents and **all three voted to raise**, and futures price roughly one-in-three odds of a September increase.

## What Happened

### NVIDIA's cost line, not its revenue line

NVIDIA (NVDA) reported after Wednesday's close, and the number to carry is a cost. Revenue was **$96,221m, up 106% on the year**; the data-centre business alone did **$89.0bn, up 117%**; gross margin printed **75.0%** and the company returned **$26.0bn** to shareholders. The September quarter is guided to **$108.0bn, plus or minus 2%, assuming zero data-centre compute revenue from China.** Earnings of **$2.46 a share are not operating earnings** — they sit 10.6% above the $2.22 adjusted figure on gains from investees who are also customers. Buried in the filings is the thing the headline missed: **supply commitments went from $119bn to $279bn in a single quarter, "primarily related to the procurement of memory."** Raw materials nearly tripled to $11,341m while finished goods fell 22%. **The company is buying memory faster than it can turn it into product.**

Beneath the print sat the run's most consequential test, and it turned on a rule of our own. We hold a rule that halves the NVIDIA position if gross margin ever prints below 72%. **It did not fire — the print was 75.0% — and on the filed record it is not even close to arming.** The September guide is **74.0% give or take 50 basis points**, so the outermost filed floor is **73.5%, a full 150 basis points above our line**. The 71–72% figure now circulating is **management's own commentary on the earnings call**. It appears nowhere in the material-event filing, the earnings release, the furnished commentary or the quarterly report, and it is not guidance. Six conditions were tested and not one fired: data-centre growth at **+117%** against a 40% floor, top-three customer concentration at **44%** against a 58% ceiling, and the officer-purchase condition failing the other way — two executives **adopted selling plans**. Ajay Puri's retirement is not a founder transition; Jensen Huang remains in the chair.

### A three-day argument was settled, and it survived

On Monday we read the memory selloff as **capital allocation, not demand** — Samsung's board choosing a payout. Wednesday's print tests that reading and it holds, confirmed twice by methods that share no evidence. **On its call, NVIDIA said it is surrendering 300 to 400 basis points of gross margin to memory cost.** The filed record carries only the step from 75.0% to 74.0%, with no cause given. The multiyear SK hynix partnership in the same release is filed. A company does not sign long supply deals with a supplier whose product is about to get cheap. The residue in the tape sits exactly where the mechanism predicts: over the three sessions since Friday's close, **Sandisk is −6.06% and Micron −2.94% against hard-disk and nearline names at +0.81%**, a **5.31 percentage-point spread**. Samsung's board decision has no read-across to nearline disk economics, and a demand scare would have hit nearline hardest of all.

Two bounds travel with that reading and both are load-bearing. The first is that **there is no structural de-rating of memory**. From 12 August to 26 August, the dynamic-memory and flash names are **+7.26% against a semiconductor fund at −4.97%** — the best thing in the sector, and an exact inversion of the three-session ordering. Monday was a discrete few-session shock inside a strong two-week uptrend, not a level shift. The second is narrower and it costs us a leg. **Micron filed a leadership change before Wednesday's open**, giving Manish Bhatia pricing scope as president and chief operating officer. That is a dated company cause on the exact session, so **Micron's Wednesday leg is withdrawn to cause-not-established** and the case now rests more on Sandisk, which filed nothing. One fact removes the after-the-fact character: **a Bloomberg piece dated 12 August — twelve days before Monday — already named capital returns as the awaited catalyst**, with investors on the record. The market said in advance which input it was watching, and Samsung delivered that exact input.

Nothing outside the artificial-intelligence complex organised itself, and the sector funds say so: **not one of the eleven sector funds cleared 1.5%**, and the whole eleven-sector range was 2.09 points, among the narrowest of the month. Inside the complex, four thematic groups spanning three sectors moved on one factor and **no sector fund captures any of them** — networking and optics **+3.96%**, disk and nearline storage **+3.49%**, artificial-intelligence power equipment **+2.51%** with eight of eight up, and gas midstream **+3.21%**. Every layer of the capital-spending chain was bought while the one name carrying the event risk was sold. That is a positioning structure — long the read-across, flat the event — and it is the opposite of de-risking. The two coherent moves outside it were big pharma at −1.41%, which dragged healthcare to worst sector with no cause found, and the broker and crypto names at −2.45%, an almost exact give-back of Tuesday's surge in the same four names.

## Source Tone

The macro copy led on sticky inflation and buried the two facts underneath it — real spending at zero and growth halved while profits rose $400.9bn. The consumer narrative running through the wires is being carried by earnings beats that, on inspection, are paid for by tariff refunds; that is arithmetic already sitting in company disclosures, not a forecast. Then the bell rang and NVIDIA changed the subject. The release is the strongest set of numbers the company has ever filed, and the call is where management said the thing that will matter longer: it cannot get enough memory, memory costs more than it forecast, and the scarcity is caused by the buildout NVIDIA is selling into. **The market spent Monday selling memory shares on a story about their demand, and on Wednesday evening their largest customer testified against that story on its own call.** One report circulating that NVIDIA will raise server prices by more than 15% traces, through six outlets, to a single Bloomberg scoop; the mechanism is now confirmed first-hand and the magnitude is still one source. Echo is not corroboration and it has not been upgraded here.
