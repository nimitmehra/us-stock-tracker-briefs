## Rates & Macro

The inflation-stripped ten-year yield — the rate that prices far-future profit, and the one that moves the AI names — rose five basis points to 2.25 percent on Wednesday. That matters more than the headline: it is the first close inside the 2.25–2.30 percent zone that marked June's AI-trade wobble. The nominal ten-year rose four points to 4.48 percent and the two-year, the market's read on the Fed, firmed three to 4.17 percent. Inflation expectations held flat at 2.23 percent, so this was a real-yield move — it hit valuations directly, not sentiment.

Today was a headwind for the AI book, and the exit line is no longer a warning. The calibrated danger zone is the real ten-year holding above roughly 2.25 to 2.30 percent; we are now in it for the first time, with the market pricing Fed hikes rather than cuts — about 88 percent odds on a December move. The two-year, the other tripwire, sits at 4.17 percent, still short of the 4.4-to-4.5 threshold.

The macro backdrop argues the other way, which is the day's tension. Private payrolls undershot at 98,000, factory activity cooled to 53.3, and the factory input-price gauge collapsed nine points to 73 — a cooling, disinflating read that new Fed chair Kevin Warsh echoed as dovish-of-fears in Portugal. Oil down 26 percent on the month reinforces the disinflation. Yet the AI book fell anyway, on positioning, not the discount rate. The June jobs report on Thursday is the week's swing.

## What Happened

### Real rates rose; the AI-capex trade de-rated.

The inflation-stripped ten-year yield rose five basis points to 2.25 percent on Wednesday, crossing into the zone that flagged June's wobble, and the capital-hungry end of the market broke on it. The semiconductor gauge (SMH) fell 5.4 percent, its worst session in weeks, led by the names closest to the physical buildout — KLA Corporation (KLAC) down 11.8 percent, Micron Technology (MU) and Sandisk (SNDK) both down 10.6 percent, Taiwan Semiconductor (TSM) 7.0 percent, ASML Holding (ASML) 7.4 percent. This was positioning, not a demand signal: the market now prices Fed hikes, and rising real rates make debt-funded hyperscaler spending harder to underwrite. Money did not leave — it rotated into banks, software and staples. Leadership changed hands; the market did not break.

### Meta will sell its spare compute.

Meta Platforms (META) rose 8.8 percent after Bloomberg reported the company is building "Meta Compute," a cloud business to rent out surplus artificial-intelligence capacity and hosted models — a challenge to Amazon, Microsoft and the neoclouds. The tape read it as bullish: the one hyperscaler monetizing its capital spending, the mirror of the payback fear that gutted Microsoft in June. The read is genuinely two-sided. Selling "excess" compute is also an admission of overbuild, and renting capacity is a low-margin detour from Meta's high-margin advertising core. The neoclouds took the competitive threat straight, CoreWeave (CRWV) falling 13.9 percent. The price popped; the overbuild question did not go away.

### General Mills wrote down its brands.

General Mills (GIS), the packaged-food maker behind Cheerios and Blue Buffalo, booked a $2.1 billion non-cash writedown of goodwill and brand value in its fiscal fourth quarter, tipping the period to an operating loss on full-year sales down 5 percent. Management disclosed it cleanly and adjusted it out — adjusted earnings rose 27 percent and a $3 billion cost program runs to 2030. But a brand-value writedown is an admission that acquired brands are worth materially less than the book carries. It landed the same day Medicare's coverage of weight-loss drugs went live — a $50 monthly copay for up to 20 million seniors, the clearest volume catalyst yet for the drugs eating into calorie-dense food. Two sources, one bear.

## Cascade Watch

### Weight-loss drugs and a brand writedown hardened the packaged-foods bear

- **What happened:** Medicare's obesity-drug coverage went live the same day General Mills booked a $2.1 billion brand-and-goodwill writedown, and the graph's link from General Mills into the packaged-foods sector and into weight-loss-drug demand both stepped up sharply in strength.
- **Why it happened:** the coverage expansion is the largest volume catalyst yet for the drugs that suppress appetite, and the writedown is management conceding that acquired food brands are worth less — the same structural erosion, confirmed from two directions on one day.
- **How it matters:** the packaged-foods volume-pressure thesis now has a fresh, dated data point; General Mills and McCormick sit on the wrong side of it, Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk on the right side.

### The real-rate exit zone fired and rippled into the AI trade

- **What happened:** the real ten-year crossing into the 2.25–2.30 percent zone registered as a new active danger signal on the rates node, wired straight through to the AI-disruption theme.
- **Why it happened:** a higher inflation-stripped yield marks down far-future AI profit and makes debt-funded capex costlier — the mechanical trigger behind the whole day's de-rate.
- **How it matters:** this is the calibrated "when to worry" line for the AI book, and it is now live rather than approaching; the next real-yield print is the one to read.

### Memory's ties to the chip cycle refreshed — but only on price

- **What happened:** Micron's links to the semiconductor sector and to the AI theme re-activated on the 10.6 percent drop, both already at maximum strength, so the refresh was recency, not a weight change.
- **Why it happened:** the move was a positioning unwind from record highs, not a change in memory demand — so the graph logs the co-movement without hardening the underlying capex-deceleration signal.
- **How it matters:** do not read the memory sell-off as a fundamentals break; the demand-deceleration trigger stays merely watching, not fired.
