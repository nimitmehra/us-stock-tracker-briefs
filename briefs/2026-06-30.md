## Rates & Macro

The ten-year Treasury yield — the rate that prices long-dated stocks — rose six basis points to 4.44 percent on Tuesday, and the whole curve backed up with it (four to seven points across the belly and long end). The number that matters most for the AI book, the inflation-stripped (real) ten-year, rose four basis points to 2.20 percent; the two-year, the market's read on the Fed, firmed four to 4.14 percent. Inflation expectations held flat at 2.24 percent, so this was a real-yield move, not an inflation scare — which means it hit valuations directly.

Today's read for the AI book is a headwind. A rising real yield marks down far-future profit, and the entire long-duration complex should have de-rated. Instead the semiconductor-tool and memory names melted up anyway — two engines running opposite signs, which is why the derivatives market distrusts the move. On the exit line: the calibrated danger zone for the AI book is the real ten-year holding above roughly 2.25 to 2.30 percent. At 2.20 percent we now sit within five to ten basis points of it — the cushion is shrinking, not widening. Restate it plainly: one more day like today and the discount rate is at the level that ended the June de-rate.

The macro backdrop frames the risk. New Fed chair Kevin Warsh makes his first international appearance in Portugal on Wednesday, and after a hawkish June — the Fed's own rate-path projections tilted toward a hike — any hawkishness there is a direct headwind. The June jobs report lands a day early on Thursday, into a thin holiday tape. Oil eased again (Brent tracking down about 20 percent for the month) on peace-deal hopes even as the Doha diplomacy deteriorated — a divergence that is the day's real tail risk.

## What Happened

### The chip-equipment melt-up ran through a rising discount rate.

The semiconductor-equipment and memory cohort ripped to fresh highs on Tuesday even as the ten-year yield rose six basis points against it. KLA (KLAC), the process-control near-monopoly and one of our stress-tested buys, closed up 8.38 percent at a record; ASML (ASML) rose 5.65 percent and Taiwan Semiconductor (TSM) 4.94 percent, both to all-time highs, with the semis sub-sector up 3.78 percent. The spark was concrete: Samsung restarted its 1.4-nanometre foundry line with equipment orders to the tool-makers, layered on last week's record Micron print. The tell is who did not lead — NVIDIA (NVDA) rose only 2.63 percent and lagged the group again, the funding source for the rotation rather than its beneficiary. The tape is paying the picks-and-shovels while the discount rate rises underneath them.

### Apple passed a "hundred-year flood" of memory costs to customers.

Apple (AAPL), the largest consumer-hardware buyer of memory chips, fell about 6 percent intraweek after raising Mac prices as much as 20 percent and iPad prices as much as 25 percent to offset surging memory costs — a move chief executive Tim Cook, in the role since 2011, called a "hundred-year flood." The stock still closed up 2.70 percent on the mega-cap tech bid, but the price hikes carry real demand-elasticity risk. This is the day's cleanest cascade fact: the memory boom that pays Micron, Sandisk and Seagate is now showing up as a double-digit cost shock at the demand end of the chain. The same input-cost pulse the brief has tracked into off-price retail now has its source on the receipt.

### Doha talks stalled; the oil tape is pricing a peace the table would not confirm.

The US-Iran negotiations in Doha did not convene as direct talks on Tuesday; American envoys met Qatari mediators only, and Tehran said it will not return to the table until a favourable memorandum — asset unfreeze, sanctions relief — is implemented first. Israel reportedly warned that war could resume "within two days." Oil eased regardless, with Brent on track for a roughly 20 percent monthly fall on de-escalation hopes. The divergence is the whole story: the commodity market is pricing a ceasefire that the diplomatic track is quietly walking away from. If the truce breaks, the oil and shipping cascade reverses violently, and the equity relief rally is resting on a foundation the negotiators declined to pour.

## Portfolio Pulse

**Apple (AAPL).** See What Happened — the 20-to-25 percent Mac and iPad price hikes to offset memory costs are the day's lead item on this holding, and the marquee cascade fact.

**Micron Technology (MU).** No fresh company catalyst; last week's record quarter is digested and the stock rested up 0.79 percent while the read-through ripped through the tool-makers. A new overhang surfaced — a price-fixing lawsuit and scrutiny of memory pricing strategy is reportedly building. It is the classic antitrust tax on a cyclical at peak pricing; early, and worth noting but not overweighting. Our view on Micron now reads pass at a cyclical peak, not a hold — the pricing-power thesis is intact but the risk is priced.

**Bloom Energy (BE).** Rose 10.07 percent — the day's largest holding move — on an Oracle deal to supply up to 2.8 gigawatts of fuel-cell power, paired with the chief executive's statement that the company does not expect to raise equity to fund the AI-datacentre buildout. The dilution-free framing is the point: an equity raise was the market's chief fear on this name, and management just took it off the table.

**Meta Platforms (META).** Google restricted Meta's access to its Gemini models, forcing Meta back onto its own Llama stack for the affected work — marginally negative for Meta, marginally positive for Google. The rumoured multi-billion-dollar equity raise remains unfiled and single-source; do not conflate it with Alphabet's confirmed raise.

**Microsoft (MSFT).** Fell about 17 percent in June, its worst month since 2000, and sits near its one-year low — the purest expression of the market's anxiety over AI capital spending without a clear payback date. The fundamentals are not the problem: cloud growth held at 40 percent and the contracted backlog is intact. The timeline is what the market is de-rating.

**Sandisk (SNDK).** Rose 10.89 percent, the best S&P 500 stock of the year, after Bernstein lifted its price view to $3,000 on long-term supply contracts stabilising earnings. The only fresh input is analysts chasing the price — a late-cycle tell on a name up nearly 300 percent in three months.

**Seagate Technology (STX).** The lone storage holding to close red on a soaring semis day, after Fox Advisors downgraded it, warning hard-disk pricing may be running ahead of reality while a rival house raised its target. The AI-storage bid is bifurcating toward flash memory and away from disk — the duopoly trade is now contested, not consensus.

## Cascade Watch

### The AI-capex trade climbed the supply chain to the tool-makers

- **What happened:** KLA rose 8.38 percent, Lam Research 5.46 percent, Teradyne 4.45 percent and ASML 5.65 percent — the chip-equipment cohort ripped on Samsung's 1.4-nanometre foundry restart with confirmed orders to the tool-makers, on top of last week's Micron print.
- **Why it happened:** a second leading-edge foundry re-entering the race is fresh demand for the machines that build chips — process control, lithography, deposition — so the AI-spending story migrated up one rung from chip-makers to their suppliers.
- **How it matters:** three of our stress-tested buys (KLA, ASML, TSM) are in this cohort and hit highs today; the melt-up strengthens the case but leaves them at or above entry, not on sale.

### Memory-cost inflation surfaced at the consumer end

- **What happened:** Apple raised Mac and iPad prices 20 to 25 percent to offset memory costs, and Amazon's cloud arm raised its AI-chip block prices — the input-cost pulse reached the end buyers.
- **Why it happened:** the memory shortage that pays Micron, Sandisk and Seagate is now large enough that the biggest hardware and cloud buyers are passing it through rather than absorbing it.
- **How it matters:** it confirms the memory duopoly's pricing power from the demand side, and flags demand-elasticity risk for the hardware names carrying the price hikes.

### Rising real yields routed everything that pays a yield

- **What happened:** the ten-year rose six basis points to 4.44 percent, and utilities, real estate, staples, healthcare and telecom all closed red while tech melted up.
- **Why it happened:** a higher risk-free rate marks down the income streams of bond-proxy sectors directly; the move was real-yield-driven, not an inflation scare, so it hit valuations rather than sentiment.
- **How it matters:** if this extends to a second session it is a regime signal, not a data point — and every duration-sensitive holding, including the AI book, sits closer to the exit line than it did yesterday.
