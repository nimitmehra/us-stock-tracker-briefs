## Rates & Macro

The ten-year Treasury closed at **4.78%**, one basis point higher on Friday and one lower across the three sessions, from 4.79% at Tuesday's close. The inflation-stripped ten-year is the rate at which distant profits get marked down, and therefore the rate that prices the artificial-intelligence book. It sits at **2.43%**, also up a basis point on Friday and down one across the window. The two-year, which is the market's read on the Federal Reserve, closed at **4.37%**, up three basis points on Friday and down two across the window. **The whole of the window's move was real rates, not expected inflation.** Expected inflation ended where it began, so the single basis point the ten-year gave up is the one the inflation-stripped rate gave up — the identity closes with nothing left over. That is the mirror image of Tuesday, when the entire rise was expected inflation and real rates did not move at all. It makes the three sessions **a small tailwind for the artificial-intelligence book**, one basis point's worth, and Friday on its own a very small headwind.

Our exit line is the inflation-stripped ten-year holding above roughly **2.25–2.30%**. The two-year at 4.37% sits just below the 4.4–4.5% band that would mark a hawkish repricing. These are calibrations, not promises.

## What Happened

### The rate path was rewritten twice.

Christopher Waller, a governor of the Federal Reserve, told an audience on Thursday to **"give disinflation a chance."** He would be inclined to hold the Fed's main interest rate at 3.50–3.75%, he said, if the next two weeks of inflation data cooperate. Futures-implied odds of a September rise fell to **48.4%**, from about 66% on Wednesday. The ten-year fell 0.71%, bond volatility 6.31% and the dollar 0.56%; gold rose 2.87% and silver 3.48%, with the miners confirming. The S&P rose 1.06%. On Friday the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported **162,000 new jobs against a consensus of 53,000**, and the odds rebounded. Two full repricings in forty-eight hours, and the S&P finished the three sessions up 1.14%. **A brief that nets the week out describes a quiet week that did not happen.**

### The jobs blowout was restaurants and school districts.

Roughly two-thirds of Friday's headline — **101,000 of the 162,000** — was food services and drinking places plus local government education. Those are the two noisiest lines in the report. Prior months were revised up a combined 55,000, and July flipped from negative to positive. Buried underneath: **information-sector employment fell 23,000**, at computing-infrastructure providers, data processing and web hosting. Those are the firms building the data centres. Separately, on Thursday morning, the services business survey printed **prices paid at a four-year high of 72.6** — the most hawkish number of the window, on the session the market priced the rate rise out. No outlet we read worked either number. The market took its instruction from a governor's sentence and ignored the data in front of it.

### Friday's index number describes neither half of Friday.

The S&P fell 0.38% and the semiconductor fund rose 2.61%. Beneath that, **twenty-seven of twenty-seven names across memory, chip equipment, optics and data-centre power rose, and none fell** — on a session when 354 of 577 stocks fell and the median stock lost 0.39%. The four group medians sit within 1.6 points of each other. None of the twenty-seven reported results inside the window. Valuation did not sort the day either: Friday's correlation with forward earnings multiples was +0.022, against Thursday's +0.333. **The diagnostic is who did not join.** NVIDIA (NVDA) rose 0.84% and Broadcom (AVGO) 0.21%, both behind their own sector fund, because **rising memory prices are revenue at Micron Technology (MU) and a cost at NVIDIA.** The specific trigger is not established, and we are not inventing one.

### Markets pay for the guide, not the quarter.

For three runs we could not say whether this market pays for the beat, the backlog or the margin. It is none of them. **Broadcom (AVGO) said it expects to ship $350bn of artificial-intelligence chips to six hyperscale customers over two years — and fell 2.75% on Thursday.** That killed the backlog explanation. **Ciena (CIEN) delivered gross margin up 450 basis points, operating margin up 1,180, and raised its full-year outlook — and fell 10.37% the same session.** That killed the delivered-margin explanation. What survives is the **forward** margin path, and it fits all eight prints we can test. **Snowflake (SNOW) guided its operating margin up, from 13.5% to 14.5%, and rose 16.55% that Thursday; Broadcom guided its down.** Two desks were wrong; the answer was a third thing.

### One memo took a sixth of Fair Isaac.

Bill Pulte, who directs the Federal Housing Finance Agency, ordered Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac on Friday to buy mortgages underwritten on a VantageScore rather than a FICO score. The three credit bureaux, he said, **"have been overcharging Americans for far too long."** Fair Isaac (FICO) closed **down 16.68%** — its second fall of more than sixteen percent in five weeks. The bureaux fell with it and Equifax (EFX) lost 6.37%. It filed no results; this was not an earnings event, and it is the one Friday loser that says nothing about the market's preferences. What it does say: a government-backed underwriting standard is a moat one official can revoke by memo. **The graph carries no record for Fair Isaac at all, and none anywhere for regulated-monopoly risk.**
