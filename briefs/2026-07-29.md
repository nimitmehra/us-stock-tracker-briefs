## Rates & Macro

The ten-year Treasury yield, the rate that prices long-dated stocks, closed at 4.67 percent, up six basis points. The number that actually marks down far-future AI profits — the inflation-stripped ten-year — did not move at all, holding at 2.41 percent; the two-year, the market's read on the Fed, fell four basis points to 4.22 percent. So the damage today sat in the long end and in sentiment, not in the real-yield channel that de-rates growth. The thirty-year jumped eleven basis points to 5.20 percent, near levels last seen in 2007 — a bear steepener that reads as term premium and inflation fear, not tighter real money.

For the AI book this was a headwind by fear, not by valuation: the gauge that hurts high-multiple names held flat while the stock-market fear gauge (VIX) rose 13 percent to 20.66. The exit line is unchanged — the real ten-year above roughly 2.25–2.30 percent, or the two-year pushing 4.4–4.5 percent. Today the two point opposite ways: the real long yield sits uncomfortably above the band but static, while the front end eased away from danger even on a hawkish hold. Backdrop: the Fed held hawkishly, second-quarter growth and the Fed's preferred inflation gauge land Thursday, and oil actually fell over the gap — Brent down almost eleven percent on the week to $89.80 — a quiet disinflationary offset the hawks will have to weigh.

## What Happened

### The Fed held. Three regional Fed presidents wanted to hike.

The Federal Reserve held its main interest rate at 3.50–3.75 percent on Wednesday, but three policymakers dissented — all of them wanting a quarter-point hike. Beth Hammack of the Cleveland Fed, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis and Lorie Logan of Dallas each judged five years of above-target inflation reason enough to tighten; it was the first time since September 2016 that three dissenters pointed the same way. Under Kevin Warsh, the new chair, the long end broke hard: the thirty-year yield rose eleven basis points to 5.20 percent while short rates fell as next-meeting cut odds were priced out. Stocks and long bonds sold off together, the fear gauge jumped 13 percent, and last week's duration relief was gone. A hawkish hold is still a hawkish signal.

### Meta missed on profit; Microsoft beat on cloud

The two biggest names reported after Wednesday's close, so the price verdict trades Thursday, not in this tape. Meta Platforms (META) grew second-quarter revenue 28 percent to $60.8 billion — a beat — but earnings of $6.18 a share missed the $7.13 estimate, and it raised its full-year capital-spending floor again — now $135–145 billion, up from $125 billion — as free cash flow collapsed to $784 million. The shares fell about seven percent after hours toward $542, vindicating July's stress test, which cut Meta to hold on exactly this mechanism: the buildout is eating the cash. Microsoft (MSFT) told the other side — Azure up 43 percent, booked backlog up 84 percent — and rose three to four percent late. The argument the market has run for a month now splits cleanly along the earnings line.

### Memory broke; Micron fell out of its band

The chip complex shed more than a trillion dollars of value on the week, and memory was the epicentre. Micron (MU) fell 9.9 percent to $739, breaking down out of the $850–950 band where we had watched to add, and now sits about five percent above the $700 aggressive-add level. A blockbuster listing for China's CXMT, a Jefferies note that contract prices may be peaking, and a $37 million share sale by chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra all fed the derate. KLA (KLAC) beat and fell 10.8 percent on a cautious guide; the chip-equipment index had its worst month since 2008. Only Seagate (STX) bucked it, up 2.3 percent with orders booked to 2029 — storage is not memory, and the tape finally said so.

### Bloom's blowout met a brutal tape

Bloom Energy (BE) delivered its first billion-dollar quarter — revenue up 165 percent to $1.07 billion, earnings of 78 cents a share past the 42-cent estimate, full-year guidance lifted to $3.9–4.2 billion. It was an unambiguous beat-and-raise, and the AI-power demand story arrived as hard numbers rather than narrative. The tape did not care. Bloom fell 25 percent on the week inside the same data-centre-power derate that hit Vertiv and Eaton, and crossed below $180 — our first-rung add level — to close at $163.75. The kill-switch we had set at $830 million of revenue is now moot; the next add sits eight percent lower. Good print, bad tape is the whole of it.

## Cascade Watch

### SaaS won; silicon lost

- **What happened:** On the same session the semiconductor ETF fell 4.8 percent, Adobe, Workday, ServiceNow, Snowflake, Intuit and Datadog all rose 4–6 percent, and the memory and AI-power names fell 7–17 percent.
- **Why it happened:** Capital is rotating out of the silicon that builds AI and into the software that monetises it, helped by a front end that eased even as the long end sold off.
- **How it matters:** "Software over silicon" is now the dominant intra-equity trade, not a whisper; it strengthened the graph's AI-disruption links into Micron and Adobe and argues against adding memory into the derate.

### Semiconductor beats got punished

- **What happened:** KLA, Lam Research, Teradyne and Vertiv all beat and raised, yet KLA fell 10.8 percent and the equipment complex sold off inside a >$1 trillion weekly chip-cap wipeout.
- **Why it happened:** After a 70-percent run the market is repricing expectations, not demand — a China-competition and peak-margin fear the fundamentals did not confirm.
- **How it matters:** The print/price divergence is the entry signal to watch; the semiconductor-sector link into Micron is capped and firing, so treat further weakness as positioning, not thesis.

### Energy stocks rose while crude fell

- **What happened:** Brent fell almost 11 percent on the week to $89.80 and West Texas crude 9 percent to $83.72, yet energy equities rose — the energy ETF up 1.9 percent, the exploration-and-production ETF up 3.4 percent.
- **Why it happened:** Institutions bid large-cap energy as the mid-decade defensive haven on a risk-off day, decoupling the equities from the barrel.
- **How it matters:** We deliberately did not fire the oil-to-producer links — this is a haven bid, not oil beta; do not read the energy rally as a crude-price signal.
