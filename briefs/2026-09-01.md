## TL;DR

American forces struck about a hundred Iranian targets after two tankers were hit in the Strait of Hormuz. Crude rose six percent, bonds sold off, and gold fell — so the market priced war as inflation, not as fear. Everything else on the day is downstream of that.

## Rates & Macro

The ten-year Treasury closed at **4.79%, up four basis points** and at its highest in twenty months. The inflation-stripped ten-year — the rate at which distant profits get marked down, and therefore the rate that prices the artificial-intelligence book — **did not move at all, at 2.44%.** The market's expected inflation rate sat at **2.35%.** Those two add to 4.79 exactly. **The entire rise was expected inflation, and none of it was real rates.**

The shape says the same thing. The two-year rose five basis points to 4.39%, the three-year six, the five-year six — while the thirty-year rose two. **The middle of the curve moved most, which is the market repricing what the Federal Reserve will do, not what growth will be.** Futures put roughly 56 to 57 percent odds on a rate **rise** at the 15–16 September meeting, on CNBC's reading of futures pricing as of 29 August. A higher figure circulated on Tuesday through one route; we are not adopting it.

**For the artificial-intelligence book, today was neither the tailwind nor the headwind we calibrate for.** Our exit line is the inflation-stripped ten-year holding above roughly **2.25–2.30%**. It sits at 2.44% — fourteen to nineteen basis points through the top, for a tenth session, and unchanged on the day. The two-year at 4.39% is now at the bottom edge of the 4.4–4.5% band that would mark a hawkish repricing. These are calibrations, not promises.

Two pieces of the backdrop matter. The manufacturing activity survey came in at 54.6 against 55.2 expected and 55.6 in July — a miss, and a second month of slowing. On an ordinary day that pulls yields down; they rose anyway. And OPEC and its allies were *adding* barrels into the spike, with a 188,000-barrel-a-day September increase approved on 2 August and the group meeting again on 6 September. **No barrels have actually been lost, so the whole six percent is a probability estimate on future disruption** — which is why it can unwind on one de-escalation headline.

## What Happened

### Hormuz turned kinetic. Gold fell.

US Central Command struck roughly a hundred Iranian Revolutionary Guard targets on Tuesday, after two commercial oil tankers were hit by projectiles in the Strait of Hormuz. The targets were air defences, radar, mine-laying gear and anti-ship missile launchers. Axios alone reports it is the first time the US has struck Iranian tankers in retaliation for attacks on shipping, under a new "tanker for tanker" policy. West Texas crude rose 6.03% to $90.93 and Brent 5.61% to $95.57. Then the tell: **gold fell 1.85% to $4,349, silver 3.02% and platinum 3.33%**, and the dollar firmed. Bullion does not fall on a war headline unless the market has decided the war is an inflation problem rather than a danger. That is the whole session in one price.

### Energy is saturated, not muted.

The producers barely moved and it looks like disbelief. It is not. **On our desk record the Strait has been effectively closed since about 10 August**, traffic down from over a hundred vessels a day to about five. The complex has repriced that for a month. **The energy and exploration funds, Chevron (CVX), ConocoPhillips (COP), Phillips 66 (PSX), Valero Energy (VLO) and Marathon Petroleum (MPC) all closed at 52-week highs.** The sector captured 77 percent of crude's move over the month, 42 percent over the week and **21 percent today**. The capture ratio is collapsing because the disruption is already owned. There is no services-versus-producer split: one name fell materially in nineteen, and two smaller service companies beat every producer. A complex at its highs does not need to chase.

### Software sold as one thing; four names didn't.

Fifteen software and security names fell an average 4.01 percent, thirteen of them down. That uniformity is one factor, not fifteen stories — the longest-dated cash flows in the index repricing against a 4.79% ten-year. **Cloudflare (NET), our holding there, fell 6.42% with no company news of any kind**, and CrowdStrike Holdings (CRWD) fell 6.90% on the day it announced a two-billion-dollar contract milestone. **But four names in the down-list belong elsewhere.** Old Dominion Freight Line (ODFL) is freight, where none of eleven names rose. Interactive Brokers (IBKR) took a UBS downgrade that *raised* its price target. Cadence Design Systems (CDNS) and Synopsys (SNPS) carry a chip-design story nobody could date to Tuesday. When a company falls seven percent on its own good news, the news is not the story.

### Dell's close was wrong within ten minutes.

Dell Technologies (DELL) fell 6.80% into its own print. Then it reported at 16:10 Eastern, ten minutes after the bell. **Revenue was $46.97bn against $44.92bn expected, adjusted earnings $7.04 against $4.92, and the artificial-intelligence order backlog stood at $95bn.** The full-year forecast went up; CNBC and Benzinga put the shares up 6.2 percent after hours. One number ran the other way: operating cash flow fell 13 percent as net income rose 255 percent — see Action Items. Credo Technology (CRDO) fell 8.65% into its own print, reported a record quarter at 16:08, and fell a further six percent after the bell. **Two artificial-intelligence infrastructure prints, hours apart, opposite answers: backlog was bought, revenue growth alone was sold.** It is not one uniform de-rating, and the bar has just moved.

### Sacramento's bill died; the liability did not.

California's Senate Bill 492 **died in the Assembly without a vote on Tuesday**, the final day of session. Speaker Robert Rivas declined to bring it up, saying the deal "does not yet deliver the relief, accountability or meaningful reform that Californians deserve." Edison International (EIX) rose 8.93% to $58.80 and PG&E Corporation (PCG) 5.95% to $14.06, against Monday's falls of 26.7% and 20.1%. **The bill's death removed new burdens; it did not grant a backstop.** There is no liability cap, no wildfire-fund replenishment, and the strict-liability standard stands. That is why roughly two-thirds of the fall survives: Edison is still down 20.5% on the week. Neither company filed anything on Tuesday, and none was owed. **PG&E speaks to the outcome on a webcast at 08:30 Eastern on Wednesday.**
